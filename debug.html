Measurements taken on this device, because the answers differ from one to another and a desktop cannot stand in for an iPad. Nothing here changes your photos or your settings, and nothing is sent anywhere. Each test says what it means underneath the number.

Diagnostic report

Facts about this device and this build. Copy it into a message when something is wrong — it carries what a screenshot cannot.

Speed

These are the numbers that decide whether the editor feels quick on this device. They take a few seconds and the page will be busy while they run.

The button that asks whether this device can run a learned selection fetches about 60 MB from this site the first time and keeps it on the device. It draws no mask and offers no selection: it measures this device, for a selection the app may one day make.

Picture checks

These ask whether the picture is drawn the way it should be on this device's own graphics chip. Each check draws a practice photograph twice and compares the two, three times over, and says first which chip is drawing. Nothing of yours is opened. It takes a few minutes and the page will be busy while it runs; then copy the result and paste it back. The line below the buttons says which check and which run it is on, what it is doing and how long it has been going, and the copied result says how long each stage took.

No photograph picked, so the practice photograph is used. A photograph you pick is read here and goes nowhere: nothing is uploaded or saved. The copied result carries its file name, its camera and its lens, and, if you place samples on it, their names and places, and nothing else of it.

The copy carries the picked file's name and camera when a photograph was picked, and the names and places of any samples placed on it, and, for each mask fitted from them, its subject, the photograph it was fitted on, the names it was weighed against and its cues.

Separation check

This shows where the colours of your photograph fall once red and blue are swapped, and what each range does when it is turned alone. Step 1: press the first button; the page draws the photograph with the swap on and the balance the editor opens at. Step 2: say where a neutral spot (a cloud or pavement) and a sunlit leaf are, by tapping the picture or by typing the two points as percentages of its width and height. Each tap, or each pair of numbers typed, redraws the picture at once at the balance that spot sets, marks the spot with its letter in a square and says in words, in the line under the boxes, whether the spot is usable, blown out or too dark; a spot that cannot be used leaves the picture where it was. Step 3: press the second button; the page draws the photograph at three white points (the opening balance, a balance set from your neutral spot, a balance set from your foliage spot), finds the hue ranges the drawn colours fall into, paints a map of them beside each picture, and turns each range in place. It runs once, not three times; it sets no look and no control, and the figures it prints are of the drawn pixels. Nothing is saved. The sweep button, once step 1 has drawn the photograph, draws it again at a grid of white balances over the camera's whole reach and under each of the editor's five channel remaps, and prints for each setting the hue counts, the saturation spread and the share under the floor. It also splits those counts into sky (by the app's own sky selection), neutral (by saturation), foliage (by the vegetation axis) and the rest, and prints, for each setting, how far apart the sky's and the foliage's hues sit, with the neutral part as the control, and what the labelling cost. A raw photograph is drawn at the size the editor works at, a quarter of the pixels of its full size. It draws many times, so it takes a few minutes on a device; its progress line says where it has got to. It sets no look, no band and no control, and it needs no spots.

Name regions by samples

After step 1 has drawn the photograph it stands here with a sample on it to place. A sample is a circle: place it on the picture, move it, set its size, give it a name and confirm it. Several samples can share a name, and a region nobody marks is left out. Then press the count: it measures what tells the named regions apart, on every cue and every pair of names, tries what it finds on the photographs kept before, and says when a sample is too small to measure. It draws nothing again and decodes nothing again. The samples are kept in this browser under the photograph's name, their names and places are in the copied result, and nothing is uploaded or written to the photograph.

Not available yet: the count needs a photograph drawn by step 1 above. Press step 1, then place and confirm samples on the picture.

Masks made from the samples

The count also fits a mask for every placed name, weighs every counted pixel by it, and draws it over the photograph in each of its renderings with the share of each name it takes. A mask is made from the photograph itself, its own recorded data, and not from the picture as it is drawn here, so it is the same mask whatever the white balance, the exposure or the red and blue swap are set to. Masks fitted on other photographs and kept in this browser are listed here whether or not their name is placed on this one: try one to see what it would take here, then keep it for this photograph, or place samples of that name here and count again to fit it afresh from them alone. A mask kept before masks were made this way was made from the drawn picture; it is listed as such and cannot be tried.
