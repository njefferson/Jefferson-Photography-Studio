## p001 Create an Infrared Custom Profile for Lightroom & Photoshop, 2019-10-19 (updated 2024-12-08), https://robsheaphotography.com/2019/10/19/creating-infrared-custom-profile-lightroom-photoshop.html
- Camera and conversion: no body named; any raw infrared image and any camera model, the profile being specific to a camera model ("DNG camera profiles are specific to each model of camera").
- Balance: "Lightroom's color temperature slider doesn't offer enough range to set a white balance on raw color infrared images effectively." The fix is a DNG camera profile made in Adobe's DNG Profile Editor: base profile Adobe Standard for the camera, colour table 6500k, on the Color Matrices tab the White Balance Calibration Temperature dragged to -100, profile named Infrared Temp -100. In the raw editor he sets the white balance with the picker on "a neutral color on your image" or drags the Temp slider.
- Post: none beyond the profile; it points to his white-balance tips page.
- Populations: none named.
- Transfers to a dedicated-conversion mirrorless body: the method is by camera model and so applies to any model the profile editor can read; the page says nothing about a conversion type.

## p002 Photoshop Actions for Infrared, 2020-06-09, https://robsheaphotography.com/2020/06/09/photoshop-actions-infrared.html
- Camera and conversion: "infrared color photos taken with a camera converted to a 550 nm to 720 nm internal filter or with a full-spectrum camera when used with an external filter of similar value."
- Balance: first a colour profile for the camera, then a white balance for the image, in Lightroom or Camera Raw, before the action runs.
- Post: IR Channel Mixer swaps the red and blue channels "converting orange/yellow sky to teal/blue and converting teal/blue foliage to orange/yellow"; IR Invert duplicates the layer, inverts it and applies a colour blend, which "may produce a more naturally colored sky than the channel mixer method"; a hue and saturation layer variant of each; an Orton effect glow action ("Less is more").
- Populations: sky and foliage, as above.
- Transfers: the page names a 550 to 720 nm internal conversion, which covers a dedicated internal conversion in that band; it does not name a mirrorless body.

## p003 Infrared Color Swap with new Lightroom Local Hue Adjustment, 2020-06-17, https://robsheaphotography.com/2020/06/17/lightroom-color-swap-with-local-hue.html

## p005 Infrared Lightroom & Lightroom Classic Workflow with LUTs, 2020-06-29 (updated 2025-04-14), https://robsheaphotography.com/2020/06/29/lightroom-lut.html
- Camera and conversion: not stated for a body; the profile pack's two profiles "will cover a variety of infrared cut-off filters for most cameras."
- Balance: the custom DNG colour profile at Temp -100 and Temp -50, then the white balance set in the editor. The slider in Lightroom "does not provide the range we need for infrared."
- Post: a Channel Mixer layer (red output 0 red and 100 blue; blue output 100 red and 0 blue) or an Invert layer in Color or Hue blend, each exported as a 32-grid-point CUBE lookup table, then combined with the camera profile into an Enhanced Profile (xmp) with Tone Map Strength Low and ProPhoto RGB. He compares: "The Local Hue Adjustment method is easier to setup, but I think that the LUT method produces superior colors. If you prefer a high-saturation style, try the Local Hue Adjustment." With these profiles "the colors will be reversed with some tools, such as Tone Curve Channels, HSL, Calibration, and the Temp slider in Graduated/Radial/Brush filters. Colors are unaffected and work as expected in Color Grading/Split Toning tools." A 2021-06-09 note changed the LUT grid from 64 to 32 points on a measured limitation of enhanced profiles.
- Populations: none named.
- Transfers: the profile and swap method is camera-model specific and applies to any model in the pack; no statement about a conversion type.

## p010 How to Fix Infrared Lens Hot Spots, 2020-08-31 (updated 2026-10-10), https://robsheaphotography.com/2020/08/31/how-to-fix-infrared-lens-hot-spots.html
- Camera and conversion: a 590 nm infrared image is the lead example; no body named.
- Balance: after the profile and a balance on a neutral subject near the edge, a hot spot is detected by the picker: "In one of my images the edge read 3700 K and the center read 4700 K. A difference of about 1000 K between edge and center means a hot spot is shifting your colors."
- Post: inverted radial masks worked from the outside in, one per ring, correcting temperature then tint then exposure, with vibrance and saturation turned all the way up as a viewing aid ("The hot spot often shows up as several concentric rings around a bright center"); a swap profile "reverses the sliders. To make a ring bluer, you may have to move the temperature slider toward yellow." Alternatives: a black and white conversion for a saturation hot spot, and Photoshop Color Range on sampled colours with a Color Balance layer, then Exposure about a third of a stop darker.
- Aperture: "The hot spot gets stronger as you stop down. In my comparison from f/2.8 to f/22, the spot is faint wide open, then becomes more intense and more focused at each smaller aperture."
- Populations: none (artefact, not a population). Transfers: lens-dependent, camera-independent.

## p020 How to Shoot and Edit Infrared with Any Camera, 2020-12-14 (updated 2026-09-20), https://robsheaphotography.com/2020/12/14/how-to-shoot-and-edit-infrared.html (article text and the video transcript of 20:20, both read whole)
- Camera and conversion: an UNCONVERTED Fujifilm X-T2 (mirrorless) with a Hoya R72 external 720 nm filter, 14 mm; the page contrasts it with a converted camera whose internal hot mirror is removed and "can be replaced with full-spectrum passing filter or an infrared high-pass filter". He recommends "a standard 720 nanometer (nm) filter". The editing is on the X-T2 unconverted file ("X-T2 Temp -100" profile in the transcript at about 10:09 and 11:04).
- Balance: raw is recommended for "full control of the white balance while editing, which is important for infrared"; in camera he skips it ("I find setting a white balance while shooting to be a hassle"), for JPEG a custom balance on "a neutral subject, such as clouds, grass, rocks, or concrete". In the editor (transcript 7:37 to 8:32) he uses the picker "on a tree, or grass, or something in the image"; the Temp slider spans about 2,000 to 50,000 K, and "if your slider here bottoms out, you may need to create a DCP profile". After the Temp -100 profile (transcript 11:16 to 11:41): "the red is gone, the organic material, the trees are mostly white and there's a faint sort of golden color in the sky. This is exactly what we want." With the enhanced profile (16:25 to 16:40): "immediately I have a good white balance. It doesn't cap out at the bottom".
- Post: three methods in the page: black and white in Lightroom (a B&W profile, tone, a split tone of a little blue in the shadows and less yellow in the highlights); the DCP profile then Photoshop Channel Mixer swap (red output 0 red and 100 blue; blue output 100 red and 0 blue, transcript 12:36 to 13:08); a Hue/Saturation layer at about +25 for "a more natural blue" sky (13:26 to 13:39) and the reds desaturated to remove the red cast "from not only from the trees, but from the shadows as well" for "white trees and a blue sky" (14:17 to 14:34); the enhanced profile method where HSL is reversed and the colour picker is used to find the band (17:44 to 18:13) and Calibration hues of reds and blues are tweaked (19:07 to 19:17). Contrast, clarity, dehaze and vibrance are added at the end (16:45 to 17:33).
- Populations: "organic material, such as trees and grass, will appear bright white. Skies, in-organic material, and shadows, will appear much darker" (transcript 3:01 to 3:15); after the swap, the sky teal and the trees with "a little bit of color"; granite is neutralised when the sky is moved to a natural blue (18:25 to 18:32).
- Aperture: one stop wider than usual landscape because "Diffraction has a bigger impact in infrared".
- Transfers to a dedicated-conversion mirrorless body: the editing and balance steps are shown on an unconverted body with an external 720 nm filter; the page makes no claim about an internal conversion.

## p022 Introduction to Infrared Photography, 2021-04-02 (updated 2026-10-10), https://robsheaphotography.com/2021/04/02/introduction-infrared-photography.html (article, no transcript)
- Camera and conversion: his own "590 nm camera" (converted); he says "Most of my color infrared is shot at 590 nm. It requires a converted camera" and that a converted camera "can add external filters with a higher cutoff than the conversion. My 590 nm camera also shoots 720 nm, 850 nm, and black and white with lens filters."
- Styles by filter: traditional 720 nm gives white foliage and, after a swap, blue sky; deep infrared at 800 nm or higher is "usually converted to black and white, with inky black skies and water and white foliage"; "Color infrared uses filters from about 470 nm to 665 nm and keeps some visible light. After a color swap, skies are blue or teal and foliage is yellow or orange"; full spectrum "Images have a red or violet cast"; chrome "mimics Kodak Aerochrome film" with a dual band filter giving red or pink foliage.
- Balance: "In Lightroom and Photoshop, the white balance slider runs out at 2000 K (or 50,000 K in the other direction), which isn't enough range for infrared." Fix once per camera with a custom profile at calibration -100; then "White balance on something neutral: white, gray, or foliage, which appears white in infrared. There is no single correct Kelvin value for infrared."
- Post: contrast with contrast, clarity, curves or dehaze ("Infrared captures a narrower range of wavelengths, so it has less natural contrast"); optionally the channel mixer swap (red output 0 red and 100 blue, blue output 100 red and 0 blue); hue and saturation to taste.
- Populations: "Foliage reflects infrared strongly, which is why leaves turn white. After a color swap in editing, the sky turns blue."
- Transfers: the page describes a general conversion-class taxonomy and states that a 590 nm conversion is his own; it names no body.

## p034 White Balance on Green Leaves with 550 nm Filter, 2022-02-14, https://robsheaphotography.com/2022/02/14/white-balance-green-550nm-infrared.html (a question and answer page)
- Camera and conversion: high-pass filters of 550 nm to 740 nm, converted or external; no body named.
- Balance (his stated rule and its reason): "You can white balance on green leaves with high-pass filters from 590-740 nm. It would be best to avoid white balancing on green leaves with high-pass filters using a cut-off of 560 nm or lower. Avoid foliage for white balancing with these filters and use color-neutral subjects for white balancing." Reason: "High-pass filters with a cut-off of 560 nm or lower allow some visible green light to pass. Foliage is no longer color-neutral ... since it will reflect green light in addition to infrared. White balancing on green foliage only works well with high-pass filters that do NOT capture visible green light. When the green light is blocked, foliage only reflects infrared light, which is color neutral." The page's comparison images: a custom balance set on grass at left and on pavement at right.
- Populations: green foliage as the balance subject, and pavement as the neutral alternative. Transfers: stated by cut-off wavelength, not by body, so it applies to a dedicated conversion of a cut-off from 590 to 740 nm; for this app's camera the conversion cut-off is not stated here.
