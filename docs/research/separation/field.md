## Bhattacharyya distance
Source: Bhattacharyya distance, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhattacharyya_distance.
Measure of separation: for two distributions it is the negative natural log of the Bhattacharyya coefficient, the coefficient being the sum over bins (or integral over the domain) of the square root of the product of the two densities; the coefficient lies from 0 to 1 and the distance from 0 to infinity. For two normal distributions the page gives one quarter of the squared mean difference over the sum of the variances, plus one half the log of the sum of variances over twice the product of standard deviations; for multivariate normals it gives one eighth of a squared Mahalanobis term under the averaged covariance, plus one half the log of the determinant of the averaged covariance over the root of the product of the two determinants.
Operations and costs: it names none for parting classes. It states that the sample coefficient is an estimator whose quality depends on the bin count: too few bins overestimate the coefficient, too many underestimate it. It states the distance is not a metric (no triangle inequality) and that the Hellinger distance, one minus the coefficient under a root, does obey it. It states the distance bounds the Bayes error rate between the stated lower and upper forms.
Assumptions: the Gaussian forms assume normal classes; the binned form assumes nothing about shape. The page says the squared Mahalanobis distance is a special case when two normal classes share variance, and that with similar means but different variances the Mahalanobis distance is near zero while the Bhattacharyya distance is not. It states no band count and no correlation assumption.
Bearing: it is defined on any space where a histogram or density exists, so it can be taken over a hue histogram, a hue-and-saturation histogram, a chroma plane or the raw channels; the page itself speaks of feature selection, image processing, speaker recognition and genetics.

## Hellinger distance
Source: Hellinger distance, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellinger_distance.
Measure: half the integral of the squared difference of the square roots of the two densities, equal to one minus the integral of the root of the product; with the factor one half it lies from 0 to 1, and the page says that without the factor it runs from 0 to the square root of two. It is a bounded metric, an f-divergence, and is related to the Bhattacharyya coefficient as one minus that coefficient under a root. The page gives closed forms for two normals, two multivariate normals, exponential, Weibull, Poisson, beta and gamma pairs, and the inequality that ties it to total variation.
Operations and costs: none for parting classes. Assumptions: none for the discrete form; the closed forms assume the named family. Bearing: defined on any binned or density-bearing space, hue histogram or plane alike.

## Mahalanobis distance
Source: Mahalanobis distance, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahalanobis_distance.
Measure: the distance of a point from a distribution with mean and covariance, the quadratic form of the difference with the inverse covariance (the page says the square roots are always defined because the covariance is positive semi-definite; the extracted text of the displayed formula lost its root sign, so the formula's own root is taken from that sentence); the page states it equals Euclidean distance after a whitening transformation, is unitless and scale-invariant, and takes the correlations of the data into account. For a normal distribution its square follows a chi-squared distribution with as many degrees of freedom as dimensions.
Operations and costs: it names the minimum covariance determinant and minimum volume ellipsoid estimators as more robust to outliers, and says the sample mean and covariance tend to be more reliable with small and biased data sets. It states that with a covariance matrix not of full rank the samples are first projected onto their affine span.
Assumptions: needs a covariance estimate per class; the classification use computes a covariance for each class and assigns a point to the class with least distance. For this camera the covariance of the three channels is near-singular because of the correlations above; the page states a singular covariance is handled by projection onto the span, and does not discuss near-singular conditioning.
Bearing: the raw channels or any plane; the page notes Mahalanobis separation is the same-variance special case of the Bhattacharyya distance.

## Directional statistics
Source: Directional statistics, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directional_statistics.
Content: angles need their own statistics because the page's own example is that 180 degrees is not a sensible mean of 2 and 358 degrees. The first trigonometric moment of a sample is the mean of the unit vectors; its length R-bar (0 to 1) measures concentration and its argument is the mean angle. The circular variance is one minus R-bar; the circular standard deviation is the root of minus twice the log of R-bar, chosen so that for a wrapped normal it estimates the underlying standard deviation; the circular dispersion is one minus the square of R over twice the square of R. The von Mises distribution has density proportional to the exponential of kappa times the cosine of the angle from the mean, normalised by a Bessel function of order 0, and is called the circular normal; kappa zero is the circular uniform. Wrapped normal, wrapped Cauchy and wrapped Levy forms are given. For multimodal data the page says median and mode are not useful when data are dispersed or multimodal. Tests named: Rayleigh for a unimodal cluster, Kuiper for possibly multimodal data.
Costs and assumptions: the mean angle is undefined when R-bar is zero; no band count is involved.
Bearing: the hue axis of a hue-and-saturation plane. The page speaks only of angles and says nothing of saturation or chroma magnitude.

## Circular mean
Source: Circular mean, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_mean.
Content: the mean of angles is the argument of the mean of their unit vectors, atan2 of summed sines over summed cosines. The page's example: the arithmetic mean of 0, 0 and 90 degrees is 30, the vector mean is about 26.565; for 355, 5 and 15 degrees the arithmetic mean is 125 and the right answer is 5. The resulting radius is 1 when all angles agree and 0 when they are uniform, in which case there is no circular mean; the page says no continuous mean operation on the circle can be defined. The circular mean minimises the sum of one minus the cosine of the difference, and maximises the von Mises likelihood for the mean parameter.
Bearing: hue only. A mean of a bimodal hue population has small R-bar; this agrees with what IR-SCIENCE.md section 6 states about a whole-frame mean hue, which the repository's own file measured as 162 against 35 degrees on one frame.

## Earth mover's distance
Source: Earth mover's distance, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mover%27s_distance.
Measure: the least cost of turning one distribution into another, mass moved times distance moved; over probability distributions it is the Wasserstein metric W1. For a one-dimensional array of bins the page gives a running-sum scan: carry the surplus bin to bin and sum the absolute carried amounts. For unequal total mass it names partial matching (not a true distance, no triangle inequality) and a creation-and-destruction penalty alpha that gives a true distance. It states the cost of the general computation is super-cubic in the number of bins for an arbitrary ground distance, solved as a transportation problem. Image use: comparing colour histograms of two images, with the ground distance chosen by the user (the page names the RGB cube as one region). The page does not describe a circular ground distance.
Bearing: on a hue histogram the ground distance has to be circular for the measure to be about hue; the repository's section 6 already states a circular form for its agreement walk. The page is about comparing two distributions, not about parting two classes in one image.

## Otsu's method
Source: Otsu's method, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otsu%27s_method.
Content: one threshold on an intensity histogram chosen to minimise within-class variance, or equivalently to maximise the between-class variance, which equals the product of the two class weights times the squared difference of the class means. It is a one-dimensional discrete analogue of Fisher's discriminant analysis and equals a globally optimal k-means on the histogram. The page says it performs well when the histogram is bimodal with a deep sharp valley, and badly with heavy noise, small objects, inhomogeneous lighting and larger within-class than between-class variance; its model is two normal distributions of equal variance and equal size, though the page says it may still work when that fails. Variants: the two-dimensional form pairs a pixel's value with its neighbourhood mean to do better on noisy images; the Kittler-Illingworth minimum-error form handles unequal sizes and variances but adds parameters and the page says it can be unstable; the iterative triclass form is for weak objects, and the page says the plain method biases toward the class with the larger variance.
Bearing: a threshold on one axis, which could be a saturation, a chroma or a hue-histogram axis; the page contains nothing about circular axes (see the circular-histogram thresholding item in the closing list).

## Whitening transformation
Source: Whitening transformation, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitening_transformation.
Content: a linear transform that makes the covariance the identity. The page lists three neighbours: decorrelation (removes correlation, keeps variances), standardisation (sets variances to 1, keeps correlation) and colouring. The whitening matrix is not unique; the page names ZCA or Mahalanobis whitening (the inverse square root of the covariance), Cholesky whitening, and PCA whitening from the eigen-system, and says the Mahalanobis distance is Euclidean after whitening.
Costs: the page states no noise cost. It says the covariance must be non-singular and that in infinite dimensions only partial standardisation is possible because the covariance operator has an unbounded inverse. Read against this camera: a whitening matrix is built from the inverse of the covariance, and with the channel correlations above the covariance is close to singular, so the inverse's large entries are what the page's non-singularity requirement points at; the page does not itself say this.
Bearing: raw channels.

## Kernel density estimation
Source: Kernel density estimation, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_density_estimation.
Content: a non-parametric density estimate formed by placing a non-negative kernel of bandwidth h on each sample and averaging. The bandwidth is a free parameter with strong influence: too small leaves spurious artefacts (the page's example uses h of 0.05 on 100 normal samples), too large hides structure (h of 2), and 0.337 is called optimally smoothed there; the limit of h to 0 is a sum of delta functions and h to infinity keeps only the kernel's shape. The page gives the mean integrated squared error criterion, the optimal bandwidth rate of n to the minus one fifth, plug-in and cross-validation selectors as the most useful over a wide range of data sets, and a rule of thumb (Silverman's) of 1.06 times the sample standard deviation times n to the minus one fifth, modified to 0.9 times the smaller of the standard deviation and the interquartile range over 1.34. The page warns that this rule of thumb can be widely inaccurate when the density is not close to normal: for a bimodal Gaussian mixture of 200 points the rule-of-thumb estimate is significantly oversmoothed. A geometric section defines local modes as points where the gradient is zero and the first Hessian eigenvalue is negative, estimated by plugging in the KDE, computed by the mean shift algorithm.
Costs: bias against variance through the bandwidth; the page states no noise cost of the kind a colour operation has. The page contains no circular kernel; its section on directions is absent (see the von Mises page below for the circular normal).
Bearing: smoothing of any histogram or sample axis, a hue histogram included only if the kernel is made circular, which this page does not do.

## Normalized difference vegetation index
Source: Normalized difference vegetation index, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalized_difference_vegetation_index.
Content: NDVI is the difference of near-infrared and red reflectance over their sum, running from minus 1 to plus 1. The page states that it is a transformation of the spectral ratio of near-infrared to visible and has no functional relationship to a spectral difference, that it is functionally but not linearly equivalent to the simple ratio, and that it equals linearly the ratio of near-infrared to the sum. It names the history (Rouse and colleagues, 1973, preceded by Kriegler and colleagues, 1969; Tucker 1979). Rationale: chlorophyll absorbs visible light from 400 to 700 nm and leaf cell structure reflects near-infrared from 700 to 1100 nm, while clouds and snow are bright in the red and dark in the near-infrared (this is the page's wording; the repository's own note is that clouds are near spectrally flat in this camera's pair). Typical values on the page: water negative or near zero, barren rock, sand or snow near zero, shrub and grassland about 0.2 to 0.4, forests approaching 1; soils 0.1 to 0.2 is marked citation needed.
Limitations stated: the sum and difference carry the same information as the two bands but the difference alone carries only part; atmospheric effects, clouds, soil moisture, anisotropy, differences between sensors' band positions and shapes, and mis-registration of the bands; derivatives named include the perpendicular vegetation index, the soil-adjusted index, the atmospherically resistant index, the global environment monitoring index and the enhanced vegetation index. It states that NDVI has also been obtained from ordinary RGB cameras by modification, giving no source on the page.
Bearing: the camera's one chroma axis is stated in IR-SCIENCE.md 4b-ix to be a red-to-infrared ratio scaled by a camera constant; this page's statement that NDVI is a transformation of that ratio is the page that bears on it. It is a single-axis index, so it speaks of the raw channels and not of a hue plane.

## Measuring Vegetation (NDVI and EVI)
Source: Measuring Vegetation (NDVI and EVI), NASA Earth Observatory, story by John Weier and David Herring, first published 30 August 2000, last updated 14 November 2025, https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/measuring-vegetation-ndvi-evi/ (the old address https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/features/MeasuringVegetation redirected here).
Content: chlorophyll strongly absorbs visible light from 0.4 to 0.7 micrometres and the cell structure strongly reflects near-infrared from 0.7 to 1.1; NDVI is near-infrared minus visible over near-infrared plus visible; the page gives very low values (0.1 and below) for barren rock, sand or snow, 0.2 to 0.3 for shrub and grassland, and 0.6 to 0.8 for rainforests, and says no green leaves gives a value close to zero. The AVHRR bands are 0.55 to 0.70 and 0.73 to 1.0 micrometres. It says the enhanced vegetation index corrects for distortions by particles in the air and the ground cover below the vegetation, and does not saturate as easily as NDVI over large amounts of chlorophyll. Clouds, aerosols, sun glare and instrument faults make daily maps patchy; longer composites remove cloud errors but remove detail, especially at high spatial resolution.
Bearing: an index of two bands; it contains no consumer camera.

## How to Interpret a False-Color Satellite Image
Source: Why is that Forest Red and that Cloud Blue? How to Interpret a False-Color Satellite Image, NASA Earth Observatory, story by Holli Riebeek, design by Robert Simmon, 14 March 2014, last updated 8 December 2025, https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/how-to-interpret-a-false-color-satellite-image/ (the old address https://earthobservatory.nasa.gov/features/FalseColor redirected here).
Content: a satellite image is built by choosing three bands and representing each in tones of red, green or blue; a false-colour image uses at least one non-visible band. The page says chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light and reflects green and infrared, and that each surface has a spectral signature; near-infrared is 700 to 1,100 nm and shortwave infrared 1,100 to 3,000 nm. Its first listed combination is near-infrared as red, red as green, green as blue: plants reflect near-infrared and green and absorb red, so plant-covered land appears deep red, denser growth darker red; cities and exposed ground gray or tan, clear water black, muddy water blue. In its Florida example with green, red and near-infrared bands, plants are dark red because the infrared band is assigned to red, faster-growing plants brighter red, water black and blue, urban areas silver. It says images from ASTER and the early Landsats are often shown in this combination because that is what the instruments measured. It also says fresh basalt lava and asphalt reflect different amounts of infrared light though they look alike in visible light.
Bearing: the false-colour convention that the repository's film note (IR-SCIENCE.md 4b) follows: bands assigned to the three display channels by position, not by hue. The page uses three genuinely separate bands and says nothing about three bands that are almost one signal.

## Landsat Normalized Difference Vegetation Index
Source: Landsat Normalized Difference Vegetation Index, U.S. Geological Survey, Landsat Missions, https://www.usgs.gov/landsat-missions/landsat-normalized-difference-vegetation-index (no date on the page).
Content: NDVI is the near-infrared minus red over their sum; for Landsat 4 to 7 band 4 and band 3, for Landsat 8 and 9 band 5 and band 4; delivered as a single signed 16-bit band with valid range minus 10,000 to 10,000 and a scale factor of 0.0001. Bearing: the same two-band index as above, with the band numbers of the satellite family.

## Red edge
Source: Red edge, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_edge.
Content: the region of rapid change in vegetation reflectance in the near infrared, from about 680 nm to 730 nm; the page says chlorophyll absorbs most visible light and becomes almost transparent beyond 700 nm, that the change can be from 5 percent to 50 percent reflectance, and that the effect accounts for the brightness of foliage in infrared photography. A citation-needed marker stands on the corner-reflector explanation. Cited: Seager, Turner, Schafer and Ford, Astrobiology 5(3), 2005. Bearing: why a 720 nm conversion samples only the foot of the edge; the page does not discuss filters.

## False color
Source: False color, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_color (read to the start of its reference list).
Content: false colour assigns colours to bands that differ from a photograph's; at least two bands are needed for a false-colour encoding and the eye's three channels are the limiting factor in combining more. The simplest encoding takes a visible-light picture and maps it differently (for example green, blue, red into red, green, blue); the traditional satellite mapping is near-infrared, red, green into red, green, blue, with the blue band unused, giving vegetation in red. It also defines pseudocolour (one channel mapped through a table or function), density slicing (a grey range cut into a few intervals each given one colour) and choropleth. It says the pseudocolour mapping should keep lightness monotonic and names the rainbow palette as an offender because its lightness goes back and forth.
Bearing: the mapping convention only; it says nothing about bands that are nearly one signal.

## Multispectral imaging
Source: Multispectral imaging, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multispectral_imaging (read to its weather forecasting section).
Content: multispectral imaging measures a small number of bands, typically 3 to 15; the page lists typical bands (blue 450 to 520 nm, green 515 to 600, red 600 to 690, near-infrared 750 to 900). It lists green, red, infrared (blue replaced by near-infrared) as a vegetation combination and says near-infrared is often shown as red. On classification it says supervised classification uses training samples of known ground truth, with the maximum likelihood principle named, and a biased selection of samples can badly affect accuracy; unsupervised classification observes the natural clustering of pixel values, k-means is named, and a threshold sets the number of classes, finer thresholds giving more classes until one class is split into several.
Bearing: the maximum-likelihood class assignment is the Gaussian class model that the Mahalanobis and Bhattacharyya pages above use.

## Von Mises distribution
Source: von Mises distribution, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Mises_distribution (read to its limiting-behaviour section).
Content: density proportional to the exponential of kappa times the cosine of the angle from mu, over 2 pi times the modified Bessel function of order 0; mu is location, kappa concentration (1 over kappa is analogous to the variance); kappa zero is uniform; for large kappa it approaches a normal with variance 1 over kappa. The circular mean is mu, the first circular moment is the ratio of the order-1 to the order-0 Bessel function, and the circular variance is one minus that ratio. It is the maximum entropy distribution for circular data given the first circular moment, and a close approximation to the wrapped normal.
Bearing: a model for a one-peak population on a hue circle.

## Topographic prominence
Source: Topographic prominence, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topographic_prominence (read to its calculations section).
Content: the prominence of a peak is the least drop in height needed to get from the summit to any higher terrain; equivalently its height above the lowest contour line that encircles it and no higher summit. For every path to higher terrain take the lowest point, the key col is the highest of these, and prominence is the peak's height minus the key col's. The highest peak's prominence equals its elevation. The page says prominence is used as a cutoff to decide which summits count as independent, with cutoffs differing between lists (15 m, 150 m, 300 ft), and that a high cutoff favours isolated peaks while a low one admits many that may be viewed as insignificant.
Bearing: the definition that the signal-processing pages below apply to a one-dimensional signal; on a circular histogram the page's terrain would need a wrapped array, which this page does not address.

## find_peaks
Source: scipy.signal.find_peaks, SciPy v1.18.0 Manual, SciPy community, copyright 2008, https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.find_peaks.html.
Content: finds local maxima of a one-dimensional array, a peak being a sample whose two direct neighbours are smaller; for a flat peak the middle sample's index is returned. Conditions can be given as half-open or closed intervals: height, threshold, distance, prominence, width, plateau size; they are evaluated in the order plateau size, height, threshold, distance, prominence, width. For distance, smaller peaks are removed first. The page warns that for noisy signals peak locations can be off and suggests smoothing the signal before searching, or other methods; it warns of unexpected results with NaNs. The wlen argument limits the area used for prominence and width.
Bearing: it treats the array as a line with two ends; a circular histogram's ends are neighbours, and the page does not offer a wrapped mode.

## peak_prominences
Source: scipy.signal.peak_prominences, SciPy v1.18.0 Manual, https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.peak_prominences.html.
Content: prominence is the vertical distance between the peak and its lowest contour line. Strategy stated: extend a horizontal line from the peak left and right until it reaches the window border or meets the signal again at the slope of a higher peak (a peak of the same height is ignored); take the minimum on each side as that side's base; the higher of the two bases marks the lowest contour line; prominence is the peak height minus it. A window length wlen restricts the area, can speed the calculation, and may stop the algorithm finding the true bases if they lie outside the window, giving a smaller prominence; the page says this can be used intentionally to calculate local prominences, and that in practice it only matters for the highest set of peaks. A peak index that is not a valid local maximum returns prominence 0 with a warning. It cites the Wikipedia topographic prominence article.
Bearing: the same one-dimensional, two-ended array as above.

## Jeffries-Matusita distance, as stated in a feature-selection paper
Source: Friedman, Singer and Rabin, Graph-based Extreme Feature Selection for Multi-class Classification Tasks, arXiv 2303.01792, submitted 3 March 2023, https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01792 and the full text at https://arxiv.org/html/2303.01792 (abstract, introduction and section 2.1 and 2.2 read; the experiments were not read).
Measure: for one feature and two classes the JM distance is 2 times (1 minus the exponential of minus B), where B is the Bhattacharyya distance of the two classes' one-dimensional Gaussian summaries: one eighth of the squared mean difference times 2 over the sum of the two variances, plus one half the log of the sum of variances over twice the product of the standard deviations. The paper says the entries form a classes-by-classes matrix per feature. By this form the measure runs from 0 (identical class summaries) towards 2 (B large), saturating, whereas B itself is unbounded. The paper says other distances such as the Wasserstein distance may be considered instead of JM.
Operations and costs: feature selection, not an image operation; it selects a very small number of features with complementary separation strengths by diffusion maps and k-means over the per-feature matrices, and eliminates features whose matrices lie close together, which is the redundancy removal the introduction states is wanted.
Assumptions: per-feature mean and variance for each class (Gaussian). It treats each feature separately; the paper's Fisher-score and correlation-based selection paragraph says correlation-based selection picks features with low linear relation to other features. It does not combine correlated features.
Bearing: a per-axis separability number that could be computed on a hue, a chroma, a saturation or a raw channel axis one at a time.

## Reduced Jeffries-Matusita distance
Source: Lashkari and one other author, Reduced Jeffries-Matusita distance: A Novel Loss Function to Improve Generalization Performance of Deep Classification Models, arXiv 2403.08408, submitted 13 March 2024, https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08408 and https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08408 (section 5 read around the loss definition; the rest was fetched and not read whole).
Content as read: the proposed loss is the sum over classes of the target times one minus the square root of the predicted probability, called a reduced version of the JM distance. It is a neural-network classification loss, not a separability measure between image classes, and states nothing about bands, noise or colour.
Bearing: none found for a hue or chroma plane.
