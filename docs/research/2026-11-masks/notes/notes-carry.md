## What the sources say

What is matched between the first and the second picture

- PerSAM (entry 4, section 3.2, equations 3 to 6): features of the reference picture and the new picture are taken by an image encoder, "SAM's frozen backbone or other pre-trained vision models", with SAM's own image encoder the default. The reference mask picks out the n feature vectors of its foreground pixels (equation 4). Each is compared with every feature vector of the new picture by cosine similarity, after pixel-wise normalising (equation 5), giving n maps; the n maps are averaged into one location confidence map S (equation 6). So what is matched is each foreground patch of the reference, one by one, against every patch of the new picture, and the answers pooled.
- PerSAM also pools the same n vectors by averaging into one embedding of the object, "the global embedding" (equation 9, target-semantic prompting), which is added to every input token of SAM's decoder (equation 10). That pooled embedding is not used for matching; it is fed into the decoder as an extra cue.
- Matcher (entry 5, section 3.1, equation 1): an image encoder gives patch features of both pictures; the correspondence matrix is the cosine similarity between every patch of the reference and every patch of the target. The default encoder is DINOv2 ViT-L/14 and the segmenter is SAM with ViT-H (section 4.1; Appendix B). Matching is then done on the reference mask's points against the target patches by bipartite matching (section 3.2), not by a mean map.
- Matcher also matches whole masks: each mask proposal made by SAM on the target is compared with the reference mask by the Earth Mover's Distance over the patch features inside the two masks (section 3.3 and Appendix A). The cost matrix is half of one minus the similarity matrix; the reference patches are the suppliers and the proposal's patches the demanders, each with equal weight.
- SegGPT (entry 6, sections 3.1 and 3.2): nothing is matched by features and no mask is pooled to an embedding. The reference picture with its coloured mask and the new picture are stitched together and one model, a plain ViT-L with 307M parameters (section 4.2), fills in the new picture's mask as the missing half of the pair. The in-context example is what tells the model which task to do. With several examples, two ways are given: the spatial ensemble stitches the examples into an n by n grid sub-sampled to one example's size, and the feature ensemble computes the examples in a batch and averages the query's features after each attention layer (section 3.2, figure 3).
- DINOv2, from the paper's appendix (entry 7, lines 1425 to 1436): patch-level features are matched across two pictures by first detecting the foreground by a PCA threshold, then taking the Euclidean distance between patch features of the two pictures and solving an assignment problem, then keeping salient matches by non-maximum suppression (figure 10). The paper says the matches fall on "semantic regions that serve similar purpose in different objects or animals" and gives the example of a plane's wing matching a bird's wing; it says the model is robust to style (an image against a drawing) and to large changes of pose. This is shown as pictures; no score is given for it. The same appendix passage (lines 1415 to 1424) says the first PCA component separates the main object from the background and the later components fall on "parts" of objects that "match well for images of the same category".
- The DINOv2 paper's instance retrieval and its linear segmentation, with a coarse logit map brought up by plain upsampling, are in notes-features.md and are not restated.

How the match becomes a prompt on the second picture

- PerSAM (entry 4, section 3.2, "Positive-negative Location Prior", equation 7): two points are taken from the map S, the highest value as the positive point and the lowest as the negative point. Two prompt tokens, no more, go to the decoder. The paper says SAM "would tend to segment the contiguous region surrounding the positive point, while discarding the negative one's". The method section describes one such pair per new picture. The introduction says the method copes with "simultaneously segmenting several identical objects in the same image"; the method section as read does not say how a single pair gives several, so that is not described.
- PerSAM then biases the decoder's cross-attention by the softmax of S, with a balancing factor alpha (equation 8), and adds the pooled embedding to the decoder's tokens (equation 10). Neither changes the prompt; both change the decoder's computation. This means the decoder is the model's own with these two additions at run time, and no weights are trained.
- PerSAM's cascaded post-refinement (section 3.2): the first mask is fed back into the decoder along with the first pair of points; then the box that encloses that mask is added as a further prompt. Only the decoder is iterated, and the paper puts the cost at "an extra 2% latency".
- Matcher (entry 5, sections 3.1 and 3.2, figure 2): forward matching gives matched points on the target for every point of the reference mask; a reverse matching carries the matched target points back to the reference picture; a forward point is kept only if its reverse point lands on the reference mask. The kept points are clustered into K clusters by k-means++ (K is 8 in Appendix B; table 8b gives 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 clusters, and the FSS-1000 score as 78.9, 83.3, 87.0, 87.2 and 86.9). Three kinds of subset are sampled as prompts: part-level (inside one cluster), instance-level (over all matched points) and global (over the cluster centres). Many groups of such prompts are sent to SAM and each gives a mask proposal. For the part benchmarks the prompts also include the smallest axis-aligned box that holds the matched points (Appendix B). The number of prompt groups sampled is not stated on any page read.
- Matcher keeps or drops proposals by three measures: emd, purity (the number of matched points inside the proposal over the proposal's area) and coverage (the number of matched points inside the proposal over all matched points). Thresholds drop fragments, a score of the form alpha times one minus emd plus beta times purity times coverage to the power lambda ranks the rest, and the top k are merged. By controlling the number of merged masks the output can cover one instance or several (section 3.3). The coefficients and thresholds are set differently for each benchmark in Appendix B (for example alpha, beta, lambda of 1.0, 0.0, 0.0 for COCO-20i and LVIS-92i; 0.8, 0.2, 1.0 for FSS-1000; 0.5, 0.5, 0.0 for the part sets; 0.4, 1.0, 1.0 for the video sets).
- SegGPT (entry 6): no prompt is derived. The output is the mask itself, in the colour the example used. The target can be one colour or several, "e.g., segmenting several categories or objects of interest in one shot" (section 3.2).

How negative points or a rejection are derived

- PerSAM: the negative point is the lowest value of S (above). Its ablation (entry 4, the main-components ablation, called table 5 in the text and numbered table 4 on the page) gives a mean IoU of 69.1 with the positive prior alone and 72.5 with the negative point added, a gain of 3.4 as the table prints it (the paragraph prints 3.6).
- Matcher: no negative point is derived. Rejection is done by the reverse check on points and by the emd, purity and coverage thresholds on proposals (above). Its ablation of the bidirectional check (table 4b): forward only 50.6, reverse only 21.4 and both 52.7 mean IoU on COCO-20i; 81.1, 47.7 and 87.0 on FSS-1000; 73.5, 41.3 and 79.5 J and F on DAVIS 2017. The ablation of the instance-level step (table 4a): without it 29.0 (COCO-20i), 76.2 (FSS-1000), 39.9 (DAVIS 2017); with it 52.7, 87.0, 79.5.
- SegGPT: nothing; there is no negative prompt in the method as read.
- The darktable page read for the earlier note (notes-editors.md entry 9) gives negative points as the person's own shift-click; the three papers here derive or need none by hand.

What each reports when the second picture is a different scene or a different instance of the same kind

The measure in every case below is a mean intersection over union (mIoU) over the pictures of a set, as the pages print it, with the measure for the whole mask against the whole truth; for video, J and F as the pages name them. Whole-frame region size or a worst case is reported by none of them.

- PerSAM (entry 4, table 1 and section 4.1): its own set, PerSeg, holds 40 objects, each with 5 to 7 pictures "in different poses or scenes", with one picture of each used as the reference; the objects are "daily necessities, animals, and buildings". So the second picture holds the same object in another pose or scene, not another instance of the kind. Results: PerSAM 89.3 mIoU and 71.7 bIoU (the boundary measure the page cites), 0 trained parameters; PerSAM-F 95.3 and 77.9, 2 trained parameters; SegGPT 94.3 and 76.5; SEEM 87.1 and 55.7; Painter 56.4 and 42.0. By object, read from the table's flattened cells in header order: PerSAM scores 38.9 on the barn and 60.6 on the robot toy where PerSAM-F scores 97.5 and 96.7. The page says no reason for the barn.
- PerSAM on category benchmarks (entry 4, table 3): FSS-1000 81.6 (PerSAM) and 86.3 (PerSAM-F); LVIS-92i 15.6 and 18.4. PASCAL-Part 32.5 and 32.9; PACO-Part 22.5 and 22.7. Matcher's paper prints other figures for PerSAM on the same sets (entry 5, table 1: FSS-1000 71.2 and 75.6; LVIS-92i 11.5 and 12.3; COCO-20i 23.0 and 23.5); the two papers do not agree on the number and neither page says why.
- Matcher (entry 5, table 1, one example): COCO-20i 52.7, FSS-1000 87.0 and LVIS-92i 33.0, against SegGPT 56.1, 85.6 and 18.6. The abstract and section 1 give the same 52.7 and 33.0, and say the 33.0 beats "the state-of-the-art generalist model by 14.4%". COCO-20i partitions the 80 classes into four folds of 20 test classes, so the second picture holds another instance of a class not trained on as a specialist; FSS-1000 has 1,000 classes; LVIS-92i is built by the paper from LVIS with 920 classes in ten folds, 2,300 episodes per fold, "a more challenging benchmark for evaluating the generalization of a model across datasets" (section 4.2). A note on the table: the page says SegGPT's training data includes COCO.
- Matcher on parts (table 2): PASCAL-Part mean 42.9 and PACO-Part mean 34.7, against PerSAM's 30.1 and 21.2. On video (table 3): DAVIS 2017 79.5 and DAVIS 2016 86.1 J and F, against SegGPT's 75.6 and 83.7.
- Matcher's upper bound (appendix table 7d): the paper's own upper bound for the framework is 83.6 on COCO-20i and 75.4 on LVIS-92i, against 52.7 and 31.4 for the method; the page says this "indicates that the Matcher framework has more potential". The 31.4 here is on one fold and differs from the 33.0 of table 1; the page does not say why beyond that the ablations "are conducted on one fold".
- Matcher on an encoder swap (appendix table 7a): mean IoU on COCO-20i is 18.8 with MAE features, 32.2 with CLIP and 52.7 with DINOv2; FSS-1000 71.9, 77.4 and 87.0. The page's reasons: text-image contrastive training "limits learning complex pixel-level information" for CLIP, and for MAE "the patch-level features ... confuse the information about the surrounding patches", which it says it suspects.
- Matcher on size (appendix table 8a): from SAM base with DINOv2 base (180M parameters, LVIS-92i 28.6, FSS-1000 85.3) up to SAM huge with DINOv2 large (945M, 31.4 and 87.0), with intermediate sizes of 399M (29.9, 85.7) and 617M (30.4, 86.3). No size of DINOv2 below base is in the table, so the small form is not covered.
- SegGPT (entry 6, tables 1 and 2): COCO-20i one example 56.1, PASCAL-5i 83.2; FSS-1000, "not trained on" it, 85.6 with one example and 89.3 with few. Video (table 3): DAVIS 2017 75.6 J and F without any video training. Its examples-count study on ADE20K semantic segmentation (appendix table S1): one example 18.8 mIoU, two 25.0, four 28.3, eight 30.1, sixteen 31.9, thirty-two 33.0, against 39.6 for a tuned prompt; the page says "more examples significantly boost the performance, e.g., +13.1% mIoU from 1 to 16 examples" and that the examples were chosen at random from the training set over four seeds with "the best results" reported. What the page says of whether the picked examples matter: "the selection of the examples can affect performance".
- DINOv2 (entry 7): matching is shown as pictures between pictures of different style and pose and between different animals and objects; no number is given.
- None of the three papers reports a picture of a landscape region (sky, water, foliage) carried to another photograph, and none reports infrared or false colour. The SegGPT page lists "stuff" among the tasks it can do and Matcher's appendix caption of its figure 6 says "Matcher can segment various background concepts like SegGPT", but the figures themselves were not shown in the text read, so what those pictures hold is not known from these pages.

What each needs beyond the segmentation model

- PerSAM: nothing beyond SAM for the training-free form, whose location prior uses SAM's own encoder by default. The fine-tuned form, PerSAM-F, learns two weights for a sum of the three masks SAM gives, by one-shot fine-tuning on the reference picture with its mask as the truth, "within 10 seconds on a single A100 GPU" (section 3.3). Its ablation (table 5 on the page) puts the other small tunings it tried below it: prompt tuning with 12K parameters 76.5, an adapter with 196K 78.3, LoRA with 293K 90.0, three mask weights 92.9, two mask weights 95.3 (the training-free form is 89.32).
- PerSAM with a box as the reference instead of a mask (table 6 on the page): PerSAM 88.1 against 89.3 and PerSAM-F 94.9 against 95.3 mean IoU; SegGPT falls from 94.3 to 36.0 and SEEM from 87.1 to 64.9.
- Matcher: a second model (DINOv2) beside SAM, k-means++ clustering, an optimal transport solve per mask proposal (the page uses an earlier published method for the transport cost), and hand-set thresholds and coefficients that differ per task (Appendix B). No page gives a run time for any step.
- SegGPT: its own trained model of 307M parameters, trained over 9K iterations at batch 2,048 on a mix of datasets (section 4.2). No SAM is used. Its in-context tuning (section 3.3) trains a single image tensor with the model frozen to get a prompt for a dataset, a scene or a face, an optional step.
- DINOv2: the features of one model; the matching of its appendix needs a foreground detector by PCA and an assignment solve.
- Whether any of these runs on a tablet, or in a browser, is said by none of them.

What each says of its failure cases

- PerSAM (entry 4, section 1 and section 3.3): "occasional failure cases" where the object has visually distinct subparts or hierarchy; the examples named are the hat on a teddy bear, the head of a robot toy and a teapot's lid and body (the positive point on the body with the negative not excluding a platform of like colour). The paper puts the cause on mask scale: "both the local part and the global shape can be regarded as valid masks by SAM". The fix it offers is PerSAM-F's scale weights, not a better match. It also names, for the first mask, "rough edges and isolated background noises" that the cascaded refinement is for.
- Matcher (entry 5, section 3.2, section 3.3, section 5): "The encoder tends to produce wrong matches in hard cases such as ambiguous context and multiple instances" (the reason for the reverse check); "The edge features of an object extracted by the image encoder can confuse background information, inducing some indistinguishable outliers" that make false-positive masks (the reason for the instance-level step); and in its limitation statement, "relatively limited instance-level matching inherited from the image encoder, which restrains its performance for instance segmentation". Its naive version, taking the most similar patches directly as the mask, "leads to inaccurate, fragmented result with many outliers" (section 3.2).
- SegGPT (entry 6, section 4.6 and section 5): the random colouring "makes the training task inherently more difficult", and the model scores below the generalist it builds on when tuned for in-domain sets: ADE20K 39.6 against 49.9 (a 10.3 point drop as the page prints it), COCO panoptic 34.4 against 43.4 (9.0). The page also says one example is weak where several are better ("it is difficult to distinguish the instances in a crowd when using only the first frame", appendix figure S1) and that the spatial ensemble loses on a larger picture, since it sub-samples the examples (table 4a: DAVIS 2017 61.9 J and F for four examples spatially against 74.7 by feature averaging, and 70.0 for one example). The page's own sentence is that the work "does not aim to claim new state-of-the-art results".
- The Segment Anything paper's own ambiguity passages (notes-editors.md entry 13, and the note on prompting in this reading) are not restated.

## What this bears on, without choosing

The four open items after this one in the handoff, then record 111's bar, then the device's figures and the two models the app already carries.

- Regions (one thing per click joined by name). PerSAM's two-point prompt and SegGPT's several-colour mask are the two places in these pages that take more than one thing in one go, and the pages say it as an ability, not as a score: PerSAM's introduction says it copes with several identical objects and the method section describes one positive point and one negative point; SegGPT says the target "can be of a single color ... or multiple colors". Matcher's merge of the top k masks is the one place where the number of regions is a controlled setting, and the page says its choice of k is what makes the output one instance or several. What the pages carry of the matching of a region to a name is only through the reference mask the person draws.
- Edges (the cue stage at full size in a narrower band). None of the three papers adds anything for edges: PerSAM's refinement feeds the mask and a box back through the same decoder; Matcher's masks are SAM's; SegGPT's output is the model's own at an input of 448 by 448 (section 4.2). PerSAM's only boundary figure is bIoU, 71.7 and 77.9 against mean IoU of 89.3 and 95.3, and the page does not say what a point of bIoU is in pixels. The coarse grid of DINOv2's patches is notes-features.md's finding and carries here: Matcher's matching is at the patch grid of 518 by 518 on a 14-pixel patch, so its matched points are placed no finer than one patch, and SAM's decoder does the edge.
- Carrying a mask to another photograph. The three methods give three ways in: a mean of per-patch likeness maps with the highest and the lowest point as the prompt (PerSAM), a two-way patch match with clusters, sampled prompt groups and a ranked merge (Matcher), and a stitched pair that a separate model completes (SegGPT). Every one needs the reference mask to be a picture, with the second picture's result an output the person can look at, so a carried result can be shown before it is kept; none says it is shown or what it would cost to show it. Where the carried result is wrong, the pages give only retries (more examples for SegGPT, the reverse check and thresholds for Matcher, the scale weights for PerSAM-F), and none gives a hand correction on the second picture; the app's own rule, samples placed on the second picture where it is wrong, is therefore not covered by any page read.
- The editor's own mask. The darktable pages (notes-editors.md) keep the result as vector paths, carried as geometry and never rederived from the clicks; none of these three papers keeps anything but the mask picture, and the only thing carried from the reference is the mask (and, for PerSAM, the features derived from it).
- Record 111's bar (no wrong region over a hundredth of the frame, edges within four pixels, judged over the whole photograph). The papers' measures are mean IoU over objects, bIoU over objects, and J and F over video frames; each is a mean over a set, which says how good the average case is and not where the worst error lies, and IoU is relative to the area of the object and not to the frame. A mean IoU of 95.3, the best figure of the PerSeg set, still allows about one part in twenty of an object's union to disagree on average. So no figure read here can be set against the bar without the carried masks being scored over whole photographs by the app's own judging, which is the app's measurement and not a page's.
- The device's figures from record 112 (an encode of a 1,024-pixel picture in about 2.4 s and a decode in about 0.12 s on one WebAssembly thread; WebGPU offered and unmeasured; 4 cores and no threads without isolation headers; 60 MB of files kept on the device). PerSAM says its additions run in the decoder and cost 2 percent extra latency in its refinement; if its matching is done with the model's own encoder, the second photograph needs one encode of its own, which is the 2.4 s figure, and the decoder runs of one pair of points and two refinement steps are the 0.12 s figure each, by arithmetic from the paper's description and not from any page's timing. Matcher's many prompt groups mean many decoder runs, and a second encoder; its default encoder is DINOv2 ViT-L/14 and its table of sizes starts at base, while the model in notes-features.md is the small form (21 million parameters, ViT-S/14) whose file sizes that note records, so none of Matcher's figures is for a model of the size the app carries. SegGPT is one model of 307M parameters by its own account, which even at one byte each is far above the 60 MB of files the device keeps, and is read here only as a method, not as a candidate.
- The two models the app already carries: MobileSAM's encoder and decoder (the pair the device's figures belong to) and DINOv2 small (notes-features.md). PerSAM's matching reads the encoder's features and so, for the app, would read MobileSAM's encoder; whether those features match as SAM's own do is said by none of these pages. Matcher's matching reads DINOv2's patch features, the one place where a second model's features already in the app would be the matching features; whether the small form matches as the large does is not in its tables. The 11 November measure of DINOv2 small is not in any of these pages and is not restated.
