## What the sources say

### One prompt for one thing, or for many

- The task the first paper defines is to return a valid mask for a prompt. A prompt is "a set of foreground / background points, a rough box or mask, free-form text". The mask need only be a reasonable one for at least one of the things a prompt could mean (a point on a shirt may mean the shirt or the person) [9, sections 1 and 2].
- The training procedure samples prompts for one target mask at a time: with equal probability a foreground point or a box is chosen for the target mask, points are sampled uniformly from that mask, and later points come from the error region between the previous prediction and that same mask [9, appendix A, Training algorithm]. The paper says "in 11 rounds per mask" [9, section 3, Losses and training].
- Many things are asked by many prompts. The notebook's batched section takes four boxes (from an object detector) as four separate prompts and returns one mask for each, shape batch by masks-per-input by height by width [2, cells 49 to 54]; its end-to-end form takes a list of images, each with its own batched boxes [2, cells 56 to 65]. The predictor's batched call takes point_coords of shape batch by N by 2, each batch row its own prompt [3, predict_torch docstring].
- The automatic route is the same unit repeated: the model is prompted with a 32 by 32 regular grid of points, each point separately yields a set of masks, and a filter and non-maximal suppression keep the confident, stable, non-duplicate ones [9, section 4, Fully automatic stage; appendix B, Filtering and Cropping].
- The second paper does the same for video: when several objects are in one video it runs inference on each object independently, sharing only the image encoder's features and running the memory bank and the mask decoder separately [5, appendix D.1, Handling multiple objects in a video]. Its limitations passage says the model "processes each object separately, utilizing only shared per-frame embeddings without inter-object communication" [5, appendix C].
- What happens when foreground points on separate things go into one prompt is not described by any passage read. The nearest statements are the annotation guidelines' list of mask errors: a point on one orange rind where the mask covers two is an error because the mask "covers an arbitrary number of objects in the collection, and should either cover one orange rind or all of them"; a point on a lizard where the mask covers a lizard and a bird is an error; a collection such as a stack of books or a crowd is a valid object [9, appendix G, Task and Judging Mask Quality 3 of 3]. These describe what a rater marks wrong in an output, not what the model does with points on two things.
- The shipped editor read earlier (darktable) says the same in its own words: more clicks on adjacent parts extend a selection, a click on a visually separate area usually switches to a new selection, and then the page says to reset and start over [from the earlier notes, notes-editors.md entries 8 and 9].
- The script of this project builds one joint prompt per subject, every sample of that subject labelled 1 and every sample of every other name labelled 0, all in one call [10, lines 160 to 163]. No page read describes a prompt built that way.

### Positive and negative points

- A point is the sum of a positional encoding of its place and one of two learned embeddings, foreground or background [9, appendix A, Prompt encoder]. The predictor's labels: 1 a foreground point, 0 a background point [3, predict docstring]; the notebook's wording is "labels 1 (foreground point) or 0 (background point). Multiple points can be input" [2, cell 20].
- A background point excludes a neighbour: with the foreground point on a truck window and a second point on the car, the notebook sets the second label to 0 "to exclude the car and specify just the window" [2, cells 34 to 37]. A box and a background point can be combined, "to select just the truck's tire, instead of the entire wheel" [2, cells 44 to 47].
- In training, each new point is foreground if the error region is a false negative (the model left something out) and background if it is a false positive (the model took something extra), taken uniformly from that region [9, appendix A, Training algorithm]. The same rule is used for the second paper's corrective clicks, with a small probability (10 percent) that a click is drawn from the ground-truth mask regardless of the model's prediction [5, section 4, Training; appendix D.2.2].
- In the first paper's data engine, annotators used "corrective foreground/background clicks to add/remove parts of the mask", and pixel-precise brush and eraser tools for what clicks did not fix [9, section 4, Assisted-manual stage; appendix F.2, Task Formulation item 5].
- The second paper's prompts on a frame are "positive/negative clicks, boxes, or masks, either to define an object to segment or to refine a model-predicted one" [5, section 3]. When it corrects a mask made by another model with the first paper's model, it first rebuilds that mask from sampled clicks until the overlap with it exceeds 0.8, then adds the correction clicks to those clicks; the paper says this "works better than several alternatives (such as feeding the output mask as a mask prompt together with new correction clicks into SAM, or taking only the correction clicks)" [5, appendix F.1.4].

### The previous mask, given with the next click

- The input and its size: mask_input is "a low resolution mask input to the model, typically coming from a previous prediction iteration. Has form 1xHxW, where for SAM, H=W=256"; the third thing predict returns is the low-resolution logits, which "can be passed to a subsequent iteration as mask input" [3, predict docstring]. The notebook says masks come back with "low resolution mask logits that can be passed to the next iteration of prediction" [2, cell 23].
- How it is used in the notebook: points are added, "if available, a mask from a previous iteration can also be supplied to the model to aid in prediction", the mask given is the logits of the highest-scored mask ("Choose the model's best mask"), and "when specifying a single object with multiple prompts, a single mask can be requested by setting multimask_output=False" [2, cells 29 to 31 and 35 to 36].
- How the model takes it: masks are a dense prompt, fed in at 4 times lower resolution than the input picture, reduced a further 4 times by two stride-2 convolutions, mapped to 256 channels and added element by element to the image embedding; with no mask a learned "no mask" embedding is added at every location [9, appendix A, Prompt encoder].
- What the training did to make it work: the previous iteration's mask is supplied as an extra prompt as the unthresholded logits "instead of the binarized mask", and when several masks were returned the one passed on is the one with the highest predicted overlap [9, appendix A, Training algorithm]. There are 11 iterations per mask: one first prompt, 8 sampled points, and two iterations "where no additional points are sampled", one at a random place among the 8 and one always last, "so it can learn to refine its own mask predictions"; the paper reports diminishing returns after 8 points (tested up to 16) and says many iterations are affordable because the decoder needs less than 1 percent of the encoder's compute [9, same passage]. The second paper's image pre-training drops the two no-new-information iterations and uses 7 correction clicks instead of 8 [5, appendix D.2.1].
- In use outside training, the first paper's instance-segmentation route adds one refinement iteration, feeding the most confident predicted mask back with the box prompt [9, appendix D.4].
- The second paper says of its video model that the memory lets "a single click" recover an object that a separate image model plus a tracker would need several clicks to re-annotate, because the separate approach restarts from scratch [5, section 3, figure 2 caption; section 2, Interactive Video Object Segmentation]. For a single image "the memory is empty and the model behaves like SAM" [5, section 1].
- How well more points do: with three points the first paper's people-segmentation figures read 90.4 and 90.1 mean overlap against 54.4 and 55.7 at one point (feminine and masculine presentation, its table 2) [9, section 6]. For the 23-dataset suite it says the gap to earlier interactive methods shrinks as points go from 1 to 9, that "SAM is not optimized for the very high IoU regime" [9, section 7.1, Results], and that "we expect dedicated interactive segmentation methods to outperform SAM when many points are provided" [9, section 8, Limitations].

### How the three-mask output is produced and chosen

- With one output the model "will average multiple valid masks if given an ambiguous prompt", so it predicts three, which the paper says is enough for nested masks "whole, part, and subpart"; during training only the lowest loss over the masks is backpropagated, and a small head on an extra token predicts each mask's overlap with the object it covers, trained with a squared-error loss, so the masks can be ranked [9, section 3, Resolving ambiguity; appendix A, Making the model ambiguity-aware].
- A fourth output token carries the single-mask path: "Ambiguity is much rarer with multiple prompts and the three output masks will usually become similar", so "we only predict a single mask when more than one prompt is given", and this fourth mask is "never returned for a single prompt and is the only mask returned for multiple prompts" [9, appendix A, Making the model ambiguity-aware].
- The predictor says: with multimask_output true "the model will return three masks. For ambiguous input prompts (such as a single click), this will often produce better masks than a single prediction. If only a single mask is needed, the model's predicted quality score can be used to select the best mask. For non-ambiguous prompts, such as multiple input prompts, multimask_output=False can give better results" [3, predict docstring]. The notebook says the same and recommends three outputs even when one mask is wanted from a single point, choosing the highest score [2, cell 25].
- How the first paper chooses in its own tests: the most confident of the three by default; an "oracle" that picks the one closest to the ground truth, to show the effect of ambiguity [9, section 7.1; appendix D.1, Single point ambiguity and oracle evaluation]. In its automatic mode, a mask is kept only if its predicted overlap is at least 88.0, if it is stable (thresholding the logits at plus and minus one gives masks that overlap by at least 95.0), and if it covers less than 95 percent of the picture; overlapping duplicates are removed at a threshold of 0.7 [9, appendix B, Filtering and Cropping].
- The second paper: it predicts several masks on a first click; "if no follow-up prompts resolve the ambiguity, the model only propagates the mask with the highest predicted IoU" [5, section 4, Prompt encoder and mask decoder; appendix D.1]. In its image pre-training, the predicted overlap of every mask is supervised but only the mask with the lowest loss gets the mask loss [5, appendix D.2.1].
- The binary mask is made by thresholding logits at zero [9, appendix B, Filtering]. The predictor compares to the model's own mask_threshold, whose value is not in predictor.py [3, predict_torch].

### Ambiguity, parts left out, and a click on a separate area

- Ambiguity: a single point "can refer to multiple objects", and the low mean overlap at one point "is the result of ambiguity" [9, section 7.1, Task; figure 9 caption]. The second paper finds the first click often gives a part: on a benchmark of whole objects "SAM 2 often tends to segment object parts (e.g. a person's arm) on the first click", and in a fairness check "the model frequently predicts the mask for a part instead of the person" at one click, which closes at three clicks [5, appendix F.2; appendix E.1.1].
- Things and stuff: annotators "freely labeled both stuff and things", and the guidelines list "'Stuff' (the ground, the sky)" among valid objects [9, section 4, Assisted-manual stage; appendix G]. The model card says it was designed to segment stuff and things [9, appendix F, Table 9]. The second paper limits its own task to "valid objects which have a clearly defined boundary" and says it does not consider "regions without visual boundaries" [5, appendix B].
- Parts left out: the first paper's limitations say it "can miss fine structures, hallucinates small disconnected components at times, and does not produce boundaries as crisply as more computationally intensive methods that 'zoom-in'" [9, section 8, Limitations]. Its guidelines name the errors a rater marks: a mask that "misses part of an object (the mask covers only one part of a building obscured by a tree)", a missing disconnected part such as "the back half of the zebra", and "failure to consistently handle obscuring foreground objects" (include both poles or neither) [9, appendix G]. Its own measure of such faults in the automatic masks: an estimated 4 percent contain small spurious components and 4 percent small spurious holes, mended by removing components and filling holes smaller than 100 pixels [9, appendix B, Postprocessing]. The second paper says the model struggles with very thin or fine details and with nearby objects of similar appearance [5, appendix C].
- A click on a separate area: no passage of either paper describes it. The second paper's closest passage is its memory design, where a click on a later frame corrects an object already tracked [5, section 3]. The editor page read earlier says a click on a separate area usually switches to a new selection [from the earlier notes, notes-editors.md entry 9].

### What the shipped one-mask decoder file is given, against the predictor's documentation

The file is sam_mask_decoder_single.onnx, run beside mobile_sam_image_encoder.onnx by the script [10, line 94]. The script passes it these inputs and reads these outputs [10, lines 134 to 142]:
- image_embeddings, taken from the encoder run on the picture resized to a longest side of 1,024 and given as height by width by 3 [10, lines 103 to 117]. The first paper's embedding is 64 by 64 with 256 channels for a 1,024 by 1,024 input, shorter side padded [9, appendix A, Image encoder].
- point_coords of shape 1 by (n plus 1) by 2 and point_labels of shape 1 by (n plus 1), the n real points scaled into the resized picture's pixels and one extra point whose label is minus one. The predictor's documentation says labels are 1 or 0 and says nothing of minus one [3]; the README names an export script and an ONNX example notebook that were not asked [1], so where the minus-one label comes from is not read from any page. The predictor says coordinates are given in pixels of the original picture and moved to the model's frame by its own transform [3, predict]; its batched call "expects" points "already transformed to the input frame" [3, predict_torch]. The script does the latter.
- mask_input of 1 by 1 by 256 by 256, all zero, and has_mask_input of 0: the script never passes a previous mask. The predictor takes mask_input as 1 by 256 by 256 [3]; the file takes it with a flag, and the script's use is the "no mask" case.
- orig_im_size of the frame's height and width. The predictor's own post-processing scales the low-resolution masks to the original picture's size [3, predict_torch]. The script reads the returned masks as one value per pixel of the original size and takes the first predicted overlap [10, line 142], so the file returns masks at orig_im_size and one score.
- Not given and not read: a box input (the predictor takes a box of length 4 in the x, y, x, y order [3]; the file's input names as the script lists them include none for a box); a multimask switch (the script takes one mask and its first score, the predictor's switch defaulting to true [3]); and whether the file also returns the low-resolution 256 by 256 logits that the predictor's documentation says to feed to the next iteration (the script reads masks and iou_predictions only). Which output the file named single returns for a prompt of one real click plus the minus-one point is not said by any page: by the paper's design a single mask is the path for more than one prompt [9, appendix A], and the file always sees at least two points.
- The script's second arm (a model with a three-mask decoder, taking the best score) is not shipped and is not discussed here [10, lines 122 to 133].

### Which statements carry over from the first paper to the files the app ships

- The MobileSAM paper changes the image encoder only: "replacing the heavyweight image encoder with a lightweight one", distilled from the first paper's encoder by a squared-error loss on the image embeddings, without the mask decoder in the loop, so that it is "automatically compatible with the mask decoder in the original SAM"; the decoder's architecture is kept, "less than 4M parameters", and the paper says the step of fine-tuning the decoder was omitted in its version [7, abstract; sections 3.1 and 3.2; section 4.1]. Prompt encoder and decoder statements above therefore describe the same design the paper says it keeps. Which decoder weights the ONNX file holds is not said by any page.
- Its figures: encoder parameters 632 million to 5.78 million and 452 ms to 8 ms per image on one GPU; total 9.66 million parameters against 68 million for a rival; "relatively smoothly on CPU" with no figure [7, tables 3 and 6; abstract].
- Its quality figures are overlaps against the masks of the original model, not against ground truth: 0.75 against 0.72 for a coupled copy in a preliminary run on 200 samples [7, section 3.2]; 0.71 to 0.74 against a rival's 0.27 to 0.41 for a foreground and a background point placed 100 to 500 apart [7, table 7]. Its evidence for point prompts and for box prompts is two figures of examples [7, section 4.2, figures 4 and 5]. It has no test with many points, none with a previous mask, and no test against ground truth.

### The second paper, for what it adds beyond the first

- It keeps the first paper's prompt encoder ("identical to SAM's") and a decoder that "largely follows SAM", adding an occlusion head for video and skip connections from the encoder's stride-4 and stride-8 features into the decoder's upsampling, which the paper says "helps produce high-resolution segmentation details" [5, section 4; appendix D.1].
- Its image encoder is a hierarchical one, trained at 1,024 by 1,024 with the picture resized to a square rather than padded [5, appendix D.2.1], where the first paper rescales and pads the shorter side [9, appendix A, Image encoder].
- On the first paper's 23-dataset suite with the first paper's data, 1-click and 5-click mean overlap are 58.9 and 81.7 against 58.1 and 81.3, at 130.1 against 21.7 pictures a second on one A100 graphics card in the paper's own setting [5, section 6.3, table 5; appendix D.3]. With its own data mix 61.9 and 83.5 [5, table 5].
- Its multi-object, correction and memory design is for video; for one still picture it says memory is empty and the model behaves like the first [5, section 1].

## What this bears on, without choosing

- Regions (one thing per click, joined by name). The sources' unit is one prompt for one thing: many things are many prompts, each with its own mask, over one stored image embedding. The decoder is under 1 percent of the encoder's compute in the first paper [9], and the device's figure from record 112 is about 0.12 s for a decode on one thread against about 2.4 s for one encode of a 1,024-pixel picture. By those two figures, a prompt per thing costs the 0.12 s each after the one encode (ten things: about 1.2 s, by plain multiplication). The four pieces of measurement named in this piece's context (a whole kind given to one prompt, separate parts left out, a mask shifting when a far sample was added) are what the sources' own unit would lead to, and no source says what a joined result of several prompts should be.
- Edges (the cue stage at full size in a narrower band). The first paper says its masks are not as crisp as methods that zoom in [9, section 8]. The decoder's mask is a 256 by 256 grid, taken to the original size by the predictor's post-processing [3, 9 appendix A]: each cell is 4 pixels of the 1,024-pixel input and, by plain division, about 10.9 pixels of a frame 2,800 pixels wide (the size the script names). The second paper's decoder takes finer encoder features into its upsampling [5, appendix D.1]; that is stated for its own files and not for the ones the app ships. The note on edges (next in this reading) holds the sources for what is put on top.
- Carrying a mask to another photograph. The sources keep a previous mask and its clicks inside one picture, or one video where memory links frames of one scene [2, 3, 5]. None carries a prompt to a different photograph; the third note of this reading holds the sources for that.
- The editor's own mask. The first paper's annotators used corrective model clicks and also pixel brush and eraser tools [9, section 4]; the shipped editor read earlier turns a model's mask into editable shapes after one generation [from the earlier notes, notes-editors.md entry 9]. The correction a click gives is made against a mask held by the model; nothing here says how a click would act on a mask a person has edited.
- Record 111's bar (no wrong region over a hundredth of the frame, edges within four pixels, judged over the whole photograph). The papers' measure is overlap per mask, which does not carry an area bar: by plain arithmetic, a mask pair with an overlap of 0.90 may differ over a tenth of their union, which for a mask covering a fifth of the frame is two hundredths of the frame. The papers' own level for agreement is 85 to 91 percent between annotators, and 94 percent of automatic masks above 90 percent against corrected ones [9, section 5, Mask quality].
- The device's figures. Every speed in the papers is on a graphics card; the one browser figure is the first paper's 50 ms for a decode on CPU. The device's 0.12 s for a decode and 2.4 s for an encode are measured on one WebAssembly thread with 4 cores and no threads without isolation headers, WebGPU offered and unmeasured, and 60 MB of files kept on the device; no page read gives a figure for that setting, and the readme's browser demo is described as multithreaded.
