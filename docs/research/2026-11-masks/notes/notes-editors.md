## How a selection is made from points in darktable, as the pages give it

- Points: click on the subject adds a foreground (positive) prompt point; shift+click adds a background (negative) point, "exclude this area from the mask"; ctrl+shift+click clears all points and starts over; right-click applies the mask (drawn masks page).
- What is made when applied: the mask is vectorised once into a group of Bézier path shapes registered with the mask manager and kept in that form; nodes are editable, the group can be combined with other shapes by the set operators (union, intersection, difference, exclusion), and polarity can be reversed. The model is not involved after the first generation, so the mask renders as cheaply as any vector shape (drawn masks page).
- Cost on the host: the first click on a new image runs the image encoder once and caches the result on disk, kept across restarts; later clicks rerun only the light decoder for each prompt point. A change to any module that distorts or crops the image (crop, rotate and perspective, lens correction) discards the cache and the next click encodes again (drawn masks page).
- The inference runtime is ONNX Runtime, one library per process, CPU only in the bundled Linux build, DirectML on Windows, CoreML on macOS; models are ONNX files in a directory with a small config.json, are not bundled, and arrive as .dtmodel packages from the darktable-ai companion repository (how-ai-works page). The page says the CPU provider is "fine for object-mask" and slow for interactive work and for upscaling at 4x. No timing figure is given for a mask.
- Stated limits: every model has edge cases where its output will be worse than the classical alternative (unusual subjects, extreme exposures, content far from training); the manual says to keep the classical modules as a fallback. If the first click picks only part of a subject, more clicks on adjacent parts extend it; clicking on a visually separate area usually switches to a new selection, and then the page says to reset and start over and not to try to repair the mask with corrections (drawn masks page).
- Scope the manual sets itself: narrow models only, no generative editing, no cloud inference, nothing run without a user's trigger (AI overview).

## How a wrong selection is corrected by hand

darktable's AI object: add a positive click where the mask is missing something, a negative (shift) click where it spills, or clear the points and start again; once applied, edit the resulting paths node by node, combine with other shapes, reverse polarity, or use the refinement sliders (feathering, blur, opacity, contrast; notes-samples.md entry 11). The page puts the cost in plain words: when the model switches to a separate object, reset and begin over. Adobe and Capture One: not read.

## How the field scores a selection against known truth

The paper (pages 8, 9, 17, 19, 28) gives the following, as printed.

- The measure: IoU, intersection over union, between a predicted mask and the ground truth mask. The per-dataset figure is the mean of the IoU over all the objects of the dataset (mIoU); the headline figure is the mean of the per-dataset mIoUs over the 23 datasets (page 19, "Evaluation"). The Model Card lists mIoU as the measure for mask quality when prompted with points (page 28).
- The masks are binary when scored. For the point prompts the model's logits are thresholded at zero to give the binary mask (page 18, "Filtering": "the binary mask (i.e., the prediction obtained by thresholding logits at 0)").
- How points are placed in the evaluation: the default for the single-point runs is the "center" of the ground truth mask, the pixel with the largest value of the mask's interior distance transform (page 8). The standard protocol, used for runs with N points, N in 1, 2, 3, 5, 9, puts the first point farthest from the object's boundary and each later point farthest from the boundary of the error region between the ground truth and the previous prediction (page 19, "Point sampling"). A harder variant starts with a random point in the mask (page 19; figure 9d).
- Ambiguity: because one point can mean several valid objects and ground truth rarely lists all, the paper adds an "oracle" score (the best of the three predicted masks against the ground truth) and a human rating from 1 to 10, because IoU alone penalises a valid mask of a different object than the one in the truth (pages 8, 9, 23). The rating guide describes 1 as no object at all, 2 to 4 as huge errors, 5 to 6 as mostly sensible with significant errors, 7 to 9 as an identifiable object with minor boundary errors, 10 as no visible error (page 23).
- Reference figures the paper gives for what IoU levels mean: annotators' own corrected masks against SAM's automatic masks gave an IoU above 90 percent for 94 percent of pairs and above 75 percent for 97 percent of pairs, and the paper says prior work estimates inter-annotator consistency at 85 to 91 percent IoU (page 6). The paper's 1-point result averaged over datasets is low (the paper puts this down to ambiguity) and moves to the low 90s with three points for the people-segmentation subsets (tables 2 and 6, pages 8 and 19).
- Edge quality is not scored by IoU in the paper; for edges it uses the standard BSDS500 metrics ODS, OIS, AP and R50 (page 10), and the paper's own limitations passage says SAM "does not produce boundaries as crisply as more computationally intensive methods" (page 12).
- The paper says nothing of sampled pixels being scored as a measure of a mask; every score is over the whole mask against the whole ground truth.

### The overlap measure, as the encyclopedia pages define it

- Jaccard index (intersection over union): J(A, B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| = |A ∩ B| / (|A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|), from 0 to 1; 0 when the sets share nothing, 1 when they are equal. With a confusion matrix: J = TP / (TP + FP + FN), where TP is predicted positive and actually positive, FP predicted positive and actually negative, FN predicted negative and actually positive (page: "Jaccard index in binary classification confusion matrices"). True negatives do not enter it.
- Dice-Sørensen coefficient: DSC = 2|X ∩ Y| / (|X| + |Y|), or 2TP / (2TP + FP + FN). The two are exact functions of each other: J = S / (2 − S) and S = 2J / (1 + J), where S is the Dice value (both pages). The Dice page says it is used in image segmentation for comparing algorithm output against reference masks in medical applications.
- A graded (non-binary) mask against binary truth: the Jaccard page gives the weighted form, the sum over pixels of min(x, y) over the sum of max(x, y), for non-negative vectors. The Dice page gives a continuous Dice for a discrete ground truth A and a continuous map B in [0, 1]: cDC = 2|A ∩ B| / (c|A| + |B|), where |A ∩ B| is the sum over pixels of a_i b_i, |B| is the sum of b_i, and c is the sum of a_i b_i over the sum of a_i sign(b_i), with c set to 1 where that denominator is zero (source cited by the page: Shamir and others, bioRxiv, 2018). The Jaccard page's own text and the Dice page's formula for c were read as printed and not checked against their cited sources.
- What this gives the scorer of step 423: its overlap over union on a hard mask must equal TP / (TP + FP + FN) counted over every pixel of the frame, and the two checks the step may run are that value for a hand-built mask with known counts and the identity J = S / (2 − S) against a Dice computed from the same counts. For a graded mask the scorer must say whether it thresholds at one half first (the paper's practice, logits at zero giving probability one half) or uses the weighted form; the pages give both and choose neither for this app.
